# Beginner content framework

Choose a knowledge path that lets learners build simple spoken answers. Start from the category below, then adjust to the actual topic and the user's facts. One group may support one or several distinct Q&A items; question IDs and branch IDs are independent. Roughly six to nine groups works for this series, but completeness and clarity decide the count.

For a new topic, name the first mind-map dimension `What` regardless of category. Its Q&A should still be a complete idiomatic question, such as `What is a cow?` or `What is a bus?`.

Before freezing a new topic, apply [content-planning.md](content-planning.md): explicitly consider all nine knowledge dimensions, record meaningful selection/omission reasons, and validate the linked content plan. These are planning dimensions, not nine mandatory questions or branches.

## Food topics

Use the owner's revised Milk chapter as the **question logic**, not a fixed list of identical headings. Start with what the food is and where it comes from. Then cover its observable appearance and taste or texture; one or two useful nutritional facts when verified; meaningful kinds, colors, parts, or ripeness stages; how people eat, drink, or use it; where they buy and appropriately keep it; and how it grows, is made, or becomes another food. Usually choose seven to nine distinct, visually teachable groups. A food need not have every dimension: choose the ones that reveal something specific and avoid repeating the same fact across groups. Retain distinctive approved content when expanding an existing topic; consolidate a smaller older branch into a richer dimension if needed for a readable map.

For example, a banana benefits from separate peel/fruit, ripening, bunches on a banana plant, and peeling/eating; a carrot benefits from root/leaves, growth in soil, raw/cooked forms, and color varieties. Milk instead benefits from dairy varieties, serving uses, purchase/storage, and collection/processing. These examples select dimensions; they do not supply a reusable questionnaire or mandate Milk's exact group count. Do not confuse a ripe/unripe stage with a botanical variety, and do not imply every food has the same color or taste.

Keep A1 questions short enough to say aloud and answers to one or two useful sentences. The picture for each group must show every named item separately and legibly; if a `Parts` answer names several components, follow the series' enlarged local-study rule. Treat nutrition, health, storage, and processing claims as facts to verify before freezing the manifest. Use appropriate scope for packaged versus fresh food, opened versus unopened products, and raw versus cooked forms. Present the complete revised bilingual Q&A for owner approval before redrawing an existing topic's images.

## Animal topics

Build a shared knowledge path, not an identical questionnaire. Usually cover what the animal is, what it looks like, where it lives, what it eats or drinks, and a distinctive action or habit. Choose other dimensions—body parts, colors/patterns, movement, senses or abilities, sounds, or growth—only when they teach something meaningful about this animal. A pet-owner perspective may be one example, not the whole topic. Use actual biological or everyday facts; do not assume all animals share Cat's life stages, sleep hours, diet, or habitat.

Sounds are optional, not a standard animal question. Ask about them when a recognizable sound is characteristic of the animal, the English answer is accurate and useful at beginner level, and the picture can provide a meaningful cue. Cat meowing and dog barking may qualify; do not carry the question over to another animal just because it appeared in a previous topic. If sound is obscure, hard to depict, or less valuable than another fact, replace it with an animal-specific question about behavior, movement, habitat, abilities, or another suitable dimension. Do not claim an animal is silent merely because its sounds are unfamiliar. Before finalizing each group, check that its answer is accurate, distinct from other groups, beginner-friendly, and visually teachable; remove filler rather than preserving a fixed count.

Good beginner questions include “What does it look like?”, “Where does it live?”, “What does it eat?”, “What can it do?”, and “How does it grow?”. Add more specific questions only when they teach distinct information.

## Transport topics

Possible dimensions: what it is; appearance; physical parts; kinds; where it goes; who uses/drives it; how people board and ride; ticket/payment where applicable; safety; why people use it. Avoid assuming every bus is the same color, every service requires an identical ticket, or every city has the same route rules. When a local system differs, use a broad truthful sentence or state the locale. For an unspecified place, illustrate cash with neutral coins or notes without a currency symbol; do not accidentally imply a country-specific fare system.

Good beginner questions include “What is a bus?”, “Where do people wait for it?”, “What do people do before getting on?”, “Who drives it?”, and “Why do people take it?”. Keep child examples concrete. For adult speaking practice, include natural first-person situations when useful.

## Everyday objects

Build a whole-object knowledge path: what it is; observable features; materials and parts; kinds; practical uses; how to operate it; where to find/buy/keep it; care; where it comes from and how it is manufactured; and a useful extension when relevant. For a full manufactured-object topic, materials/parts and a distinct manufacturing process are required. A material question (made of), a factory location, use or cleaning cannot substitute for how it is made. Explain the process with at least two meaningful ordered actions, scoped and fact-checked for the actual object. For Mirror, ask how a common glass mirror is made as well as teaching glass, reflective backing and optional frame. Use the content-plan gate before rendering. Other dimensions remain adaptable; do not impose identical questions or a fixed count. Expand vocabulary and scenarios beyond what the learner volunteered, but do not overwrite their real details.

## Two audience levels, one visual brand

- `children`: concrete, observable facts and short sentences; Chinese meaning helps parents guide practice. Illustrations carry more of the load.
- `adult_beginner`: natural daily-speech questions, personal examples, and practical situations; still A1-friendly and illustrated. Do not make a child's simple fact sheet sound like an adult autobiography.

## Content review

- Keep definition, category scope, and quantifiers accurate. “Can” and “often” may be more truthful than “always”.
- Keep distinct facts distinct: appearance, habitat, use, and behavior should not recycle the same answer in different words.
- Check specialized numbers, life stages, health/safety claims, and time/place details against authoritative sources before including them.
- Make English simple and idiomatic, then translate the final English faithfully into Chinese. The Chinese should explain, not add a new fact.
- Freeze all approved values in the manifest. Cover, picture-only practice, Q&A, and caption must use the same order and facts.
